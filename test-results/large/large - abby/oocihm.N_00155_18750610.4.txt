Sir Henry Thompson on Vivisection.

(London Morning Advertiser, May 13th.)-

Sir Henry Thompson's opinions upon
surgical, and doubtless upon many other
matters, are entitled to be received with re-
spect. But the most learned men and the
most skilful operators are not infallible on all
subjects. Thus Sir Henry in his partial de-
fence of the demoniacl practice of vivisection
as at present conducted, has contrived to
write a little, just a very little, absolute non-
sense ; and we not only take the liberty to
tell him so, but shall proceed to prove it. Sir
Henry Thompson endeavors to show that
there are many customs annually in use
amongst us far more cruel than vivisection,
and less defensible. Now, here we join issue
with him. - We have not a word to say in de-
fence of those disgusting and cruel fly-papers
vulgarly called " catch-’em-alive ohs." We
confess that we cannot endure to see them,
and would never tolerate their use. Probably
the addition of a little poison to the glutinous
composition with which these abominable
sheets of insect torture are smeared would re-
move the objection. But does Sir Henry
Thompson pretend to say that a fly feels the
same pain as one of the higher order of ani-
mals—say, for instance, a dog ? If a dog's
leg is torn off, can he run merrily away and
feed? But if a fly chance to loses leg, it will
not prevent him from sitting on a lump of
sugar a minute after: tear a leg in the
same manner from a man, and in all
probability he dies. Again, would Sir Henry
Thompson compare smoking a wasps' nest to
the destruction of human life in the caves of
Dahra? Sir Henry cites the cruelty of the
angler. Now, a fish is far higher in the scale
of creation than a maggot or a worm. But

he tells us that worms on the fisherman's
hook suffer more than the dogs in the vivi-
sector's operating-room ; faithful creatures
whose agonies have been so graphically de-
scribed by a medical man, that it is difficult
to banish the hideous picture from the mind.
Now, with regard to a fish, we ask if a dog,
much more a human being, were to get a
flight of hooks of proportionate size to those
which are used to capture fish—of let us say
some three or four pounds weight, would that
dog, after being dragged about for a quarter
of an hour or so by these hooks fixed to a
cord, should he happen then to escape with
his throat and jaws torn, mangled and bleed-
ing, rush at and greedily devour a piece of
meat upon another similar flight of hooks, or
display any appetite at all? But this is pre-
cisely what a fish will do. Even in flv-fish-
ing many an angler has recovered his hook
in the mouth of a salmon, trout, or grayling,
lost, it may be, half an hour before, the fish
rising again to the lure. A pike has been
known during the last season to be hooked by
two anglers one after the other, and has thus
been captured, as a joint possession, by both.
There never was, in our opinion, a more mis-
taken notion than that so charmingly con-
veyed by Shakspere in the well-known pas-
sage—

" The poor beetle that we tread upon

In corporal sufferance feels a pang as great
And when a giant dies."

Of course the truth of our contradiction
must depend upon how the giants dies. If
he, too, is wiped out of existence in an instant
—say, by a railway smash—probably he feels 1
no more than the beetle. * * * * Our
opinion is that nature, whom we suppose to
follow the mandates of an all-wise Providence,
is far too merciful to place a large amount of
capacity for suffering in worms or beetles, as
we know she does not in fish. No digger
can put a spade in earth without chopping
worms in twain, and as neither part dies im-
mediately, what a frightful amount of suffer-
ing the necessary culture of the earth must
cause, according to Sir Henry Thompson and
the vivisectionists, whose cry is to all the
words—" Tuquoque." We question if lob-
sters can be much more speedily put to death
than by being plunged into boiling water;
but if Sir H. Thompson can suggest a humane
method of killing lobsters, by all means let
him do so. Doubtless there are many abom-
inable and utterly needless cruelties perpe-
trated by ignorant, thoughtless, and barbar-
ous persons. The fattening of geese for the
sake of their enlarged livers by nailing their
feet to a board and placing them before a fire,
and the plucking of live turkeys and other
poultry because the feathers come out more
easily, are barbarities worthy of all execration,
and we should like to see the perpe-
trators punished by the lash. Now

In the interval between the adjournment of
the Court yesterday evening and the meeting
this morning, I carefully read and considered
the whole evidence. The result at which I
arrived in regard to the acts of the respond-
ent and others on the polling day, and during
the hours appointed for taking the polls at
Davidson's Hotel, in the village of Carlisle,
rendered it unnecessary in my opinion to de-
termine any other of the charges advanced
for the purpose of avoiding the election. My
finding and my report to the Speaker will be
limited to that one matter.

It will be convenient to begin by referring
to the statutory provisions on which this
charge of corrupt practices is founded. They
are contained in the Ontario statutes,
32 Vic., ch. 21, sec. 66; 36 Vic., ch. 2, sec. 1
and sec. 3. sub-sec. 1 and 2.

The first of these enactments is, "Every
hotel, tavern, and shop in which spirituous
or fermented liquors or drinks are ordinarily
sold shall be closed during the day appointed
for polling in the wards or municipalities in
which the polls are held; and no spirituous
or fermented liquors or drinks shall be sold
or given to any person within the limits of
such municipality during the said period,
under a penalty of $100, in every such case."

2nd. "'Corrupt practices' or 'corrupt
practice' shall mean bribery, treating, and
undue influence, or any of such offences as
defined by this or any act of the Legislature,
or recognized by the Common Law of the
Parliament of England; also, any violation of
the 46th, 61st, or 71st sections of the Elec-
tion Law of 1868, and any violation of the
66th section of such last mentioned Act dur-
ing the hours appointed for polling."

3rd. "When it is found upon the report
of a judge upon an election petition that any
corrupt practice has been committed by any
candidate at an election, or by his agent,
whether with or without the actual know-
ledge and consent of such candidate, the
election of such candidate, if he has been
elected, shall be void." And further, when
it has in like manner been found " that any
corrupt practice has been committed by or
with the actual knowledge or (query, if this
should not be and) consent of any candidate
at an election in addition to his election, if
he has been elected, being void, he shall
during the eight years next after the date of
his being so found guilty be incapable of be-
ing elected," &c., &c.

SITUATION.

LONDON, May 31.—In the House of Lords this
evening, Earl Russell asked that the correspond-
ence between Great Britain and the Continental
Powers, arising out of the recent war alarm, be
laid before the House.

The Earl of Derby, Minister of State for the
Foreign Department, replied that he sympathized
with the curiosity of those who had watched the
course of foreign affairs during the last few weeks.
The Government desired to conceal nothing it
had done, but it would be impossible to give a fair
account without largely using confidential com-
munications relating to the policy and opinions
of other Governments, who would object to such
a disclosure. The publication of these com-
munications would prevent English diplo-
matists from receiving information in future,
and to submit a mutilated correspondence might
mislead the House. The causes of dispute were
liable to recur ;therefore it was not desirable in
the interests of peace to give publicity to the de-
bates of the controversy. Lord Derby then pro-
ceeded to state that persons highest in authority
in Berlin of late openly declared that the French
army had become a source of danger to Germany,

The operation of pitching tents was accom-
plished, considering that it must have been
new to most of the men, with propriety and
despatch, and where in the morning was an
expanse of pasture ground, in the evening
was filled with long lines of snowy tents.
The men all came in in heavy marching
order, carrying a day's rations in their
haversacks, and in consequence there needed
to be no cooking. But fatigue parties were
instantly set to work preparing ranges for
cooking and other indispensible conveni-
ences to camp life. There were no attempts
made to drill, and discipline even was not in
any sense strict. Of course the posting of
sentries was a duty that every military man
will perceive could not be overlooked, and
each battalion had its own lines somewhat
vaguely defined by the beat assigned to them.
The police department was also thoroughly
appointed, for even the fatigues of the route
were quite insufficient to tame some of the
fellows, who appeared to think that "camp"
meant a place to have a jolly good time.
There is not the shadow of a doubt that this
illusion will be dispelled before the camp is
over, and that they will return to the bosom
of their respective families wiser and better-
drilled subjects of this Dominion than they
are at present.

GRAND PIGEON MATCH FOR TWO THOUSAND DOL-
LARS AND THE CHAMPIONSHIP—BOGARDUS
THE WINNER—AN EXTRAORDINARY SCORE.

Captain A. H. Bogardus, of Elkhart, Ill.,
and Ira A. Paine, of this city, again came
together yesterday in a pigeon shooting con-
test. The occasion had long been looked for-
ward to by the large number of gentlemen of
this vicinity who knew the skill of these men
with the gun, and as the stakes were unusual-
ly large the interest became widespread and
assumed a speculative tendency. Not alone
were sporting men generally desirous of
witnessing,the decision of the long mooted
question as to which of these two crack shots
was the superior, but members of the Union
and other clubs took up the question, bet their
money and attended the shoot to see for
themselves how the contestants acted at the
score. Prospect Park Fair Grounds was the
place selected to decide the event, and at two
o’clock 500 or 600 gentlemen were there
gathered and anxiously awaiting the call of
the referee.

The match was for $1,000 a side, 100 birds
each, 30 yards rise, 80 yards boundary, five
traps and Hurlingham rules to govern,
which requires the gun to be held below the
armpit until the bird is on the wing. Paine
being the challenger allowed Bogardus $50
for expenses, which in reality made the
stakes $1,050 against $1,000. Mr. Parks was
selected referee. ° The birds were furnished
by Miles L. Johnson, and, as a lot, were
strong and rapid flyers. Bogardus used a
Scott breechloader, 13 bore at the muzzle and
12 at the breech, while Paine shot with a
Grant muzzle-loader, 11 bore, which differ-
ence of guage allowed Bogardus under the
rules 18 inches the advantage of the range,
Paine standing at the score and the Captain
having one foot advanced beyond it, the dis-
tance noted as above.

At eighteen minutes past two o’clock
Paine went to the trap, and at this juncture

the betting in the pools was :—Bogardus,
$50 ; Paine, $35. Two bets of $500 to $400
was made by the Captain’s backers. A brisk
southeast wind was blowing throughout the
shoot. Paine opened the ball and brought
down his first bird with the second barrel,
and continued with capital luck until he had
to his credit nine dead pigeons, when he
broke and missed the tenth. Meanwhile
Bogardus had secured but eight out of his
ten, one of which, shot through and
through, managed to get beyond the
boundary. By this time the general
bearing of the shooters at the score had
been compared and commented upon, Bo-
gardus receiving much commendation for his
sportsmanlike manner and the chance he in-
variably gave his birds. At the twentieth
bird Paine had grassed eighteen, while Bo-
gardus, in the same number, had scored but
sixteen, which gave Ira the lead by two
pigeons. Friends of the- latter now rushed
to the pool box and made him bring even
terms with the Captain, which, no doubt,
was very pleasing to the Western delegation.
When thirty birds had been trapped by each
the scores stood—Paine, 23 ; Bogardus, 23—
a tie—which some looked upon at this stage
of the game as being very singular. The
better informed hardly deemed it accidental,
but set it down as one of the plans of the
Captain to make the occasion more entertain-
ing. Paine cut down six in his next ten, so
did the Captain, another singular coincidence,
and Paine going off and killing ten others
dead as a stone, and Bogardus allowing three
of the number to escape, the score at the
fiftieth bird stood—Paine, killed, 39 ; missed,
11. Bogardus, killed, 36 ; missed, 14. This
put the former three birds ahead, half-way
around the game, and many imagined it
looked serious for the Western champion.
His friends, however, were buying all the
pools they could secure, having kept him the
favorite at $50 to $10. A rest of twenty
minutes was now taken and the guns were
cleaned.

ADMIRAL OSBORNE.

During the last month the scientific world
has lost three prominent men, whose deaths
have not been generally noticed in the press.
The first of these is the English Admiral,
Sherard Osborne, one of the Arctic navigators
of the past. He entered the Royal navy at
the age of 15, and afterwards served on the
East India and China stations until 1843. In
1849 he was a volunteer in the expedition
which in that year left England in search of
the lost ships of Sir John Franklin. His zeal
and intrepidity in boat and sledge expeditions,
and in the exploration of the Arctic coasts
of Melville Bay, were recognized by his
appointment to the command of the Pioneer,
which, in 1852, took part in a second
and more extended expedition to the same
region. He served in the Black Sea during
the Russian war, was at the bombardment of
the Chinese forts in 1857, landed the English
expeditionary force in Mexico in 1861, and
suppressed piracy in the Chinese seas in
1862. He retired on half-pay in 1864.- He
has long been a member of the Royal Geo-
graphical Society; and was the prime moving
cause of the resolution to fit out the Alert
and Discovery for the new Arctic expedition.
He was not only an able seaman, but was
also well known in the world of letters, and
was a frequent contributor to the Transac-
tions of the Royal Geographical Society and
Blackwood.

A. G. FINDLAY.

Alexander Findlay, the geographer, was
almost as well known in America as in Eng-
land. He was born in London Jan. 6, 1812.
His earlier days were occupied in geographi-
cal and hydrographical compilations in the
form of maps, atlases and charts. About
1834, he constructed his well-known atlases
of ancient and comparative geography. One
of his first works of sterling worth to the
maritime world was the " Directory for the
Coasts and Islands of the Pacific Ocean." In
1858, he commenced, and before his death
accomplished, the great work of preparing his
series of "Nautical Directories " for the whole
world, comprising nearly 6,000 pages, which
are now accepted as standard authority the
world over. The London Atheneum says of
his incidental labors :

Ocean currents was a subject to which he
gave great thought, endeavoring to unravel
the system of Nature's laws for the use and
advantage of the seamen ; and the Journal of
the Royal Geographical Society is indebted
to his fertile brain and ready pen for some
valuable contributions to its contents on this
as well as other subjects. Although Mr.
Findlay's works are principally hydrographi-
cal, yet he took part in geographical discus-
sions, and devoted much time to his friend
Dr. Livingstone’s labors, for whose spirit of
determination he had much admiration—
indeed, the question of the sources of the
River Nile was the last subject of his careful
investigation. In Arctic matters he took a
deep interest at the time of Sir J. Franklin's
catastrophe, sifting the sad question of his
route in an able paper, contained in the
twenty-sixth volume of the Society’s Journal.

SAMUEL HEINRICH SCHWABE,
the astronomer, died recently. In December,
1827, he discovered the eccentricity of Saturn's
ring with respect to the planet. In 1835, he
made a series of physical observations of
Halley’s comet, but his principle astronomi-
cal work was his long course of observations
of the solar spots, whence was led to the re-
markable discovery of their periodicity in
number and frequency about every ten or
eleven years. For this he received the gold
medal of the Royal Astronomical Society of
London in 1857.

GEORGE ELIOT.—Mrs. Henry M. Field
wrote of George Eliot : The appearance of
George Eliot, or Mrs. Lewes, has been often
described. There was a very good portrait of
her in the exhibition last year at the Royal
Academy. No-one who had ever seen her
could mistake the large head (her brain must
be heavier than most men’s), covered with a
mass of rich auburn hair. At first I thought
her tall; for one could not think that such a
head could rest on an ordinary woman's
shoulders. But, as she rose up, her figure
appeared of but medium height. She received
us very kindly. In seeing for the first time
one to whom we owed so many happy hours,
it was impossible to feel towards her as a
stranger. All distance was removed by her
courtesy. Her manners are very sweet, be-
cause very simple, and free from affectation.
To me her welcome was the more grateful as
that of one woman to another. There is a
sort of free masonry among women, by
which they understand at once those with
whom they have any intellectual sympathy.
A few words, and all reserve was gone.
"Come, sit by me on this sofa," she said, and
instantly seated side by side, we were in deep
conversation. It is in such intimacy that
one feels the magnetism of a large mind in-
formed by a true woman's heart ; then as the
soul shines through the face, one perceives its
intellectual beauty. No portrait can give the
full expression oT the eye, anymore than of the
voice. Looking into that clear, calm
eye, one sees a transparent nature, a soul of
goodness and truth, an impression which is
deepened as you listen to her soft and gentle
tones. A low voice is said to be an excellent
thing in woman. It is a special charm of the
most finely cultured English ladies. But
never did a sweeter voice fascinate a listener
—so soft and low that one must almost bend
to hear. You can imagine what a pleasure it
was thus to sit beside this gifted woman, and
hear her talk of questions interesting to the wo-
men of England and America. But I should
do her great injustice if I gave the impression
that there was in her conversation any
attempt at display. There is no wish
to "shine." She is above that
affectation of brilliancy which is often
mere flippancy. Nor does she seek to
attract homage and admiration. On the con-
trary, she is very averse to speak of herself,
or even to hear the heartfelt praises of others.
She does not engross the conversation, but is
more eager to listen than talk. She has that
delicate tact—which is one of the fine arts
among women—to make others talk, suggest-
ing topics the most rich and fruitful, and by
a word drawing the conversation into a chan-
nel where it may flow with a broad, free cur-
rent. Thus she makes you forget the cele-
brated author, and think only of the refined
and cultivated woman. You do not feel awed
by her genius, but only quickened by it, as
something that calls out all that is better and
truer. While there is no attempt to impress
you with her intellectual superiority, you feel
naturally elevated into a higher sphere. The
conversation of itself floats upward into a
region above the commonplace. The small-
talk of ordinary society would seem an imper-
tinence. There is a singular earnestness
about her, as if those mild eyes looked deep
into the great, sad, awful truths of existence.
To her life is a serious reality, and the gift
of genius a grave responsibility.

THE BRITISH ARMY.— Some time since
during the administration of Mr. Gladstone,
the Queen issued a warrant abolishing the
purchase of army commissions. Under the|
old system an English officer could select
any regiment he pleased by paying a certain
sum of money. This act cost the British gov-
ernment millions of dollars, because the gov-
ernment indemnified the officers for the
privilege of which it deprived them. Mr.
Disraeli’s government restored the system.
The effect of this is, as Lord Cardwell, the
Secretary of War under Mr. Gladstone, showed
in the House of Lords debate, that the " poor
officers will do all the disagreeable work and
the rich officers fill the pleasant posts."

LIGHTHOUSE Illumination.—The necessity
of intense illumination for lighthouses has
induced the English Government to under-
take various experiments with a view to
realize this object. At one of their coast
stations there are employed for this purpose
two magneto-electric machines, each being
put in motion by a two-horse power steam
engine, and, excepting, wear and tear, the
whole consumption of material to produce
the light is the coke and water required to
raise steam for the engine and carbon points
for the lamp in the lantern. The lamp is a
delicate arrangement of machinery, hold-
ing the two carbons between which the
electric light exists, and regulating their ad-
justment, so that while they gradually con-
sume away the place of the light shall not
be altered. The electric wires end in the two
bars of a small railway, and upon these the
lamp stands. When the carbons of a lamp
are nearly gone that lamp is lifted off and
another instantly pushed into its place. The
light never goes out through any deficiency
or cause in the engine and machine house,
and when it has become extinguished in the
lantern a single touch of the keeper's hand
sets it shining as bright as ever, sending
forth its illumination with a power of bril-
liancy far surpassing that of any other fixed
light.

Several heads of mummies were shipped
from Egypt, to Halifax, England. They
were packed in separate boxes, and one of
them got soaked in salt water on the voyage.
This particular head when taken out was
found to have recovered its natural outlines,
the sea water having had an astonishing
effect. M. D. Conway, who was present, says:
"For a few minutes we saw an unmistakably
Egyptian countenance, with pleasant compos-
ed expression—the face of a man who lived
at least three thousand years ago. We could
only say that the powerful aromatic gums which
had been used had arrested the decay which
the salt water had now rendered possible, and
which speedily set in after the head was taken

The 39th Norfolk Rifles came in late last
night, and slept on the bare ground; a pretty
hard piece of furniture, some of them said,
but they will get blankets immediately. The
Battalion looks well.

The Welland Battery came in late last
night, and the men are at work on guns and
accoutrements, cleaning up. The officers
are Captain King, commanding, Lieutenant
McCracken ; Dr. Mack, Surgeon, and
seventy men and fifty horses. There is still
one battery and one troop to report them-
selves: they are expected to-night. The
entire force of all arms will be on the ground
to-morrow, when the actual figures will be
obtained.

There are in the general orders for to-
morrow, among others relating to mere rou-
tine, an order requiring officers commanding
corps to have a medical inspection made and
report. Another order, that officers will be
held responsible if any spirits are sold in the
canteens within their lines. And another,
that all stragglers will be arrested by the
military police. These are salutary orders,
and strike at existing irregularities. The
police quarters are in the Old Barracks, near
the jail, and is composed of forty men de-
tailed from the battalions.

Serious disturbances have ocuurred at
Plusnitz, in the Province of Western Prussia,
on the occasion of the installation of a Roman
Catholic clergyman, who has subscribed to
the new ecclesiastical laws imposed by the
Government. A crowd of Ultramontanes
belonging to the parish besieged and eventu-
ally demolished the viearage, carrying off
the newly-installed vicar by force beyond the
parish boundary. The people also seized the
keys of the church. Military assistance had
to be summoned to quell the riot.
